Europass CV: What It Is, and When You Should Use It

Updated November 2026 · 6 minute read

Europass is a free CV format and online profile tool run by the European Commission. It was introduced in 2005 so that qualifications and experience could be described the same way across European countries. It is useful in a few specific situations and a poor choice in many others.

The short version

  • Use Europass when the application explicitly asks for it.
  • That mostly means EU-funded programmes, some public bodies and some universities.
  • For private-sector jobs, a normal one or two page CV usually serves you better.
  • Europe has no single CV standard. Conventions change by country.

What a Europass CV contains

You create it on the official Europass website, either as a saved profile or as a one-off document. The sections are fixed: personal information, work experience, education and training, language skills, digital skills, and optional extras such as publications, projects and volunteering.

The language section is the part people most often borrow. It uses the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR), which grades listening, reading, speaking and writing from A1 (beginner) to C2 (near-native). Stating "German: B2" tells a European employer far more than "German: good", and you can use CEFR levels on any CV.

When to use it

  • The advert or application portal says "Europass CV" or "CV in Europass format".
  • Applications tied to EU funding, such as Erasmus+ placements and some research or training projects.
  • Some public-sector and university applications, particularly in southern and eastern Europe.

If the instructions say Europass, follow them exactly. Submitting a different layout can get an application rejected on formal grounds.

When not to use it

Many private-sector recruiters dislike Europass CVs. The template spends a lot of space on labels and layout, so a career that fits on two pages elsewhere can run to four. Because every Europass CV looks alike, yours is harder to remember. And the format encourages listing duties rather than achievements.

For a company job in Germany, the Netherlands, Ireland, France, Spain or the Nordic countries, a normal CV written to local conventions is the stronger choice unless the advert says otherwise.

Language

Write the CV in the language of the job advert. If the advert is in English, send English. If it is in German, a German CV is expected, and sending English suggests you cannot work in German. State your level in each language with a CEFR grade, and be accurate: interviews often switch language to check.

Privacy

European CVs traditionally carried more personal data than British or American ones, but that is fading. You are not obliged to give your date of birth, marital status or nationality anywhere in the EU, and a growing number of employers prefer not to receive them.
